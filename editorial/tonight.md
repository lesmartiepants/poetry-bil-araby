# Tonight

Open with **“I Pass by the Dwellings” · أمرّ على الديار** and Darwish’s **“To My Mother.”** Follow them with the eleven approved selections from readers’ saves, then Siraj’s 36 short picks in his selected order. Each can be read on its own, with one thing to notice before opening Insights.

| Night | Poem                                                                         | Why Tonight                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| ----- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1     | Majnun Layla, _I Pass by the Dwellings_ (86021)                              | The walls are what grief can still touch. The Arabic keeps returning to the word for the dwellings, as though the speaker were making one more pass.                                                 |
| 2     | Mahmoud Darwish, _To My Mother_ (85954)                                      | There’s a quiet ache in the way Darwish remembers his mother through bread, coffee, and touch. The longing for Palestine takes on the intimacy of an ordinary morning at home.                       |
| 3     | Mahmoud Darwish, _Bind My Shackles_ (85310)                                  | The notebooks and cigarettes give the opening the plain detail of a search. As Darwish turns to the body, he imagines how much harder it is to take away a voice.                                    |
| 4     | Ibn Mayyada, _The Tears of the Eyes and the Sparkle of Water_ (20480)        | Ibn Mayyada gives water a face that can laugh and weep at once. In four short lines, the poem changes our sense of how much water a tear can hold.                                                   |
| 5     | Al-Mutanabbi, _A Plea for Grace_ (27314)                                     | Al-Mutanabbi lets us hear a tipsy guest trying to preserve his dignity. His praise grows so elaborate that the smallness of what he wants is part of the joke.                                       |
| 6     | Al-Hallaj, _The Secrets of Passion_ (6818)                                   | Al-Hallaj starts by speaking of “Him” and suddenly turns to “You.” One pronoun is enough to bring an unseen beloved into the room.                                                                   |
| 7     | Izz al-Din Ibn Maʿqil al-Mahlabi, _The Impact of Separation_ (86330)         | Grief drains the color from the speaker’s hair just as the departing caravan floods the poem with red. The farewell is told in colors the eye can still hold after the people are gone.              |
| 8     | Mahmoud Sami al-Baroudi, _Generosity of Soul, Nobility of Character_ (84469) | Al-Baroudi begins like a man defending his accounts. The poem asks what wealth is worth when someone close to you needs it.                                                                          |
| 9     | Nizar Qabbani, _A Homeland Rejecting Love_ (87778)                           | Qabbani uses the language of a police report to describe a country’s pursuit of love. The chill comes from how ordinary the verbs sound.                                                             |
| 10    | Abu al-Aswad al-Duʾali, _The Distancing Friend_ (89072)                      | A whole friendship is measured in arm’s lengths and outstretched hands. Every attempt at closeness gives the other person a new distance to put between them.                                        |
| 11    | Lisan al-Din ibn al-Khatib, _Throbbing of the Wounded Heart_ (87669)         | Ibn al-Khatib makes separation a bodily ailment, with a pounding heart and a wounded eyelid. The speaker judges the health of his love by a different measure.                                       |
| 12    | Nizar Qabbani, _Complaint to the Heavens_ (87139)                            | Qabbani addresses the sky as though it could settle a private grievance. The repeated “how” lets hurt turn, almost in the same breath, into wonder.                                                  |
| 13    | Mahmoud Darwish, _Childhood Memories_ (85970)                                | Darwish puts the windows of a childhood home and the cold window of a prison into the same inventory. The repeated “I have” lets the speaker claim a life large enough to hold both.                 |
| 14    | Ibn al-Fāriḍ, _Yearning for the Beloved_ (63382)                             | Longing changes the size of the world in this little poem. As the earth closes in, the soul seems to have room for just one thing.                                                                   |
| 15    | Al-Sharīf al-Murtaḍā, _Time's Sharp Blades, Its Ways_ (87183)                | Al-Murtaḍā gives Time sharp blades and then lets it fall ill like one of us. The change makes it feel both more dangerous and more familiar.                                                         |
| 16    | Abu al-Hasan al-Jurjani, _Baghdad's Longing_ (88933)                         | The rain over Baghdad takes on the direction and force of the speaker’s tears. His longing is so strong that he starts bargaining with the very days that kept him away.                             |
| 17    | Abu al-Hasan al-Jurjani, _Farewell to the Group_ (88871)                     | At the caravan’s farewell, sunset and dawn seem to occupy the same instant. The faces begin to shine just as they are about to disappear behind their curtains.                                      |
| 18    | al-Wa'wa' al-Dimashqi, _Tears of Exile_ (87530)                              | Al-Waʿwaʾ begins with a stranger weeping and ends by making the eye itself a place of exile. The image puts an entire sea inside the smallest part of the face.                                      |
| 19    | Ibn Zaydūn, _Solace of Time and Sun of Life_ (85509)                         | Ibn Zaydun gives hope the shape of work done with the hands. The image of planting makes what he eventually harvests all the harder to bear.                                                         |
| 20    | Amir al-San'ani, _A Call to the Graves_ (88474)                              | The person who cannot sleep calls out to the dead as though they were sleeping. The farewell is tender enough to wish them the one thing grief has denied the living.                                |
| 21    | Abu Ishaq al-Ilbiri, _Fleeting Joys, Perishing Bodies_ (85579)               | Al-Ilbiri carries the dead to their graves with his own hands and still feels strangely far from what he witnesses. The poem catches the uneasy distance between knowing something and taking it in. |
| 22    | Ibrahim bin Sayyar al-Nazzam, _The Trace of a Touch_ (86226)                 | A look leaves a mark and an imagined handshake hurts. Al-Nazzam makes desire so vivid that thought itself seems capable of touching a body.                                                          |
| 23    | ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, _Wonders of the World, Safety's Marvel_ (87373)          | The first couplet treats safety as the strangest thing in the world. That reversal sets the pace for two more familiar words the poem asks us to reconsider.                                         |
| 24    | Abu Firas al-Hamdani, _A Smile of Sweet Nectar_ (85646)                      | Daisies, dawn, and wine all arrive in one smile. Abu Firas lets the senses run together until the beloved seems to make the whole morning possible.                                                  |
| 25    | Abū al-Aswad al-Duʾalī, _Flood of Generosity_ (86416)                        | The poet answers a rebuke with an ocean and a rain cloud. Giving feels as natural here as weather, and just as difficult to argue with.                                                              |
| 26    | Ghayda' al-Ayyubi, _Spirit of Poetry_ (88873)                                | Here a song can melt rock and make a river overflow with fragrance. The images ask us to imagine a poem powerful enough to change the material world.                                                |
| 27    | al-Hallaj, _Breeze and the Beloved_ (85479)                                  | Al-Hallaj sends a breeze to deliver a message about a thirst that only grows when he drinks. The body gives us a way to feel a longing it cannot satisfy.                                            |
| 28    | Abu al-Hasan al-Jurjani, _Heart's Longing for the Beloved_ (88589)           | In these four lines, the speaker is ready to forgive time itself if it brings one place close again. The bargain gives an almost legal shape to an entirely private longing.                         |
| 29    | Abu Nuwas, _The Living Dead_ (85692)                                         | Abu Nuwas imagines the part of himself that is still alive standing beside the part that has died. The strangeness of that image gives his final prayer a very human vulnerability.                  |
| 30    | Abu al-Atahiya, _Tears for Lost Youth_ (43335)                               | Growing old feels to Abu al-Atahiya like watching a branch stripped of leaves. He gives youth the tenderness of a person he would like to see once more.                                             |
| 31    | Abū al-Baqāʾ al-Rundī, _A Stream and a Breeze_ (85389)                       | Al-Rundi takes the hard rings of armor and remakes them out of ripples. By the time darkness comes, even the difference between the stream and the sky feels uncertain.                              |
| 32    | Qays ibn al-Mulawwaḥ, _A Dove's Call in the Night_ (86163)                   | A dove wakes the poet and immediately makes him doubt his own grief. The bird has become the measure of what he can honestly say about loving Layla.                                                 |
| 33    | al-Qadi al-Tanukhi, _Generosity and Wise Words_ (89059)                      | The rain cloud is embarrassed by this man’s generosity. Al-Tanukhi keeps looking for something large enough to measure what a human being can give.                                                  |
| 34    | Muwaffaq al-Din ibn Abi al-Hadid, _The Soul Scattered Between Lands_ (86827) | The speaker’s body, resolve, and heart live in three different cities. The poem makes a map out of the parts of himself he can no longer keep together.                                              |
| 35    | Abu al-Ala al-Ma'arri, _Adrift in Life's Deep Sea_ (87286)                   | Al-Ma’arri gives the soul a house and then imagines what can happen to it when the tenant leaves. The image lets him ask about death as plainly as someone looking at a building.                    |
| 36    | Qays ibn al-Mulawwaḥ, _Love's Fatal Grip_ (88897)                            | The speaker wakes the sleepers to ask whether love can kill. The poem grows more unsettling as a question about the body turns into a question about guilt.                                          |
| 37    | Abū al-Baqāʾ al-Rundī, _Sunlit Face, Lunar Grace_ (43398)                    | Al-Rundi puts an eclipse on a face. A few beauty marks are enough to make looking at a person feel like looking up at the night sky.                                                                 |
| 38    | Imam al-Shafi'i, _Ward Off Worries_ (87505)                                  | Some eyes sleep while others stay open over things that may never happen. The poem gives worry a bodily cost before it offers the sleepless person any comfort.                                      |
| 39    | al-Abbas ibn al-Ahnaf, _Departure Without Farewell_ (47679)                  | There is time for one last look and no time for a goodbye. The poet makes the eyes do the work that words can no longer do.                                                                          |
| 40    | Abu al-Atahiya, _The Erosion of Days_ (86248)                                | Abu al-Atahiya imagines day and night as a millstone that never stops turning. Gray hair becomes the visible dust left by all that quiet grinding.                                                   |
| 41    | Imru' al-Qays, _Nectar's Wine, Honey's Melt_ (74289)                         | Wine, rain, lavender, and honey pass through the same image. Imruʾ al-Qays makes a trace of a mouth arrive with the abundance of a whole landscape.                                                  |
| 42    | Abu Bakr al-Sanawbari, _The Bitter Kiss of Parting_ (59663)                  | The silence in the lovers’ eyes is as palpable as the stillness of the embrace. Then the poem gives distance a hand and lets it pour the drink.                                                      |
| 43    | Ibn Abd Rabbih, _Exile of the Soul_ (85330)                                  | Ibn ʿAbd Rabbih starts by naming the soul’s loneliness, then corrects himself mid-line. For a moment we can feel him trying to work out which part of him has really been exiled.                    |
| 44    | al-Hallaj, _A Love of Fire and Paradise_ (6768)                              | Al-Hallaj gives the beloved the warmth of fire and the promise of paradise in one breath. The words for sin and shame begin to change meaning under the pressure of that devotion.                   |
| 45    | Majhul, _The Hardships of Life's Journey_ (86759)                            | A desert becomes a sea and a rib cage becomes a place to keep a fire. The poem makes the burdens of the road and the burdens we carry in ourselves feel unexpectedly close.                          |
| 46    | Dhu al-Nun al-Misri, _The Trace Remains_ (14242)                             | The hand that writes will turn to dust while the words may remain. In four lines, the poem makes that survival feel like a responsibility.                                                           |
| 47    | Abu Ishaq al-Sabi, _A Beloved's Protection and Loyalty_ (88494)              | Someone steps between the speaker and the heat. What begins as a small act of care becomes a little puzzle about how one sun can shelter us from another.                                            |
| 48    | Majhul, _Spring's Scent, Beloved's Face_ (87340)                             | A rose can be smelled, a cheek can be kissed, and the poem keeps letting the two touch. By the last cup, even the speaker has trouble telling the sources of his delight apart.                      |
| 49    | Muḥyī al-Dīn ibn ʿArabī, _The Heart's Life in Your Words_ (87325)            | Ibn ʿArabi uses the image of Jesus raising the dead to describe what a word or a glance can do to a heart. The miracle makes the silence at the start of the poem feel especially sharp.             |
