# Editorial guide for Poetry بالعربي

## Our ambitions

1. **Make Arabic poetry accessible.** Remove the linguistic, technical, and educational barriers to reading it.
2. **Make Arabic poetry desirable.** Create an experience that inspires people to seek out another poem, another poet, another voice.
3. **Make Arabic poetry part of the world’s shared literary imagination.** Let someone with no connection to the Arab world discover that al-Mutanabbi, al-Khansa, or al-Sayyab can articulate something about their own life.

The third ambition defines the project. We want a 22-year-old in Montreal, Tokyo, Mexico City, or San Diego to encounter al-Ma’arri and be astonished that someone living a thousand years ago was wrestling with questions they have today.

We want someone going through heartbreak to discover Ibn Zaydun. Someone questioning the meaning of ambition to discover al-Mutanabbi. Someone struggling with grief to discover al-Khansa. Someone trying to understand where they belong to discover Darwish.

And we would want them to discover those poets not primarily because they are Arabic, but because they are extraordinary.

**The ultimate measure of success is whether a reader leaves with an Arabic poem that now belongs to them, too.**

## How we choose poems

We want readers who are new to Arabic literature to leave their first poem curious about the next one. **Give them something to notice before giving them something to know.** Start with an image, a sound, or a turn in a line. Bring in history and form when it changes what they hear on a second reading.

Build around poems that have mattered to generations of readers and poems that make people want to read aloud. Put famous works beside discoveries and classical voices beside living ones. Across the catalogue, vary place, period, form, difficulty, and mood. Let women shape the main reading path as well as the collections. Keep the Arabic central, with a readable English translation and a way to hear the original.

## The editorial role

Take the role of a literature professor who instills wonder and excitement in students encountering Arabic literature from outside the Arab world. Assume curiosity and intelligence, and explain unfamiliar language, forms, and history as they become relevant. Read aloud, ask a question the poem can answer in more than one way, then invite a rereading. Choose works that earn the reader’s attention for their beauty, depth, or surprise. Anchor the introduction in recognizable poems and poets with historical weight, and bring living poets into the conversation from the start.

## Worked example · Start with the wall

_I’d begin with a man kissing a wall._

In the short poem traditionally attributed to Majnun Layla, he passes the places where Layla lived and kisses one wall, then another. Even in translation, the gesture is startling. Then the poem makes you reconsider what you saw. In a classroom, I’d read it aloud and ask, **“What can a place hold after someone has left it?”** That question can carry a reader from Imruʾ al-Qays’s abandoned campsite to Dunya Mikhail’s search for a country. It also makes _I Pass by the Dwellings_ a strong opening Tonight pick. [Arabic text and translation](https://www.luciaillanes.de/es/arabic_poems/amurru_3ala) · [Study of the attribution](https://knowledge.uchicago.edu/nanna/record/726/files/Yadgar_uchicago_0330D_13328.pdf?registerDownload=1&version=1&withMetadata=0&withWatermark=0)

Use the same observation at three scales. **Tonight** offers the gesture and leaves its meaning for the reader to discover. **The journey** comes back to the question of home across centuries. **The collection** uses a closer pairing, such as Al-Khansa and Fadwa Tuqan, to show how a later poet can inherit and change a way of writing about loss.

## What each surface offers

| Surface                | Editorial job                                                                                                                                                                                                         |
| ---------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Tonight**            | One poem for everyone each night, complete as a reading experience. Rotate familiar works and discoveries. Give the reader one brief cue in **Why Tonight** and save historical context for Insights.                 |
| **Journey**            | A short, optional sequence with an arc. Each step should change how the reader hears a previous poem or prepare them for the next. Give each transition a reason and let readers skip ahead.                          |
| **Collection**         | A shelf with a clear editorial question. Readers can dip in, read in order, or return later. Show range across voices or forms and give each selection a reason to belong.                                            |
| **Pathways and doors** | Follow a specific connection from the poem just read, such as a shared image, a change in form, an answer to a poet, or a different take on the same feeling. Personalize these choices while keeping Tonight shared. |

## Before a selection goes live

- Record the corpus ID, poet, Arabic incipit or title, source edition, and any attribution or textual variants. Label excerpts and record where they begin and end.
- Read the Arabic aloud and review the English line by line. Credit the translator and confirm permissions for the text, translation, and any recording we use.
- Write one reading cue and one reason for the placement. Check whether the cue spoils the poem’s turn and whether the placement adds something to the sequence or shelf.
- Check the result in the app with the actual text. Confirm order, credits, and any Tonight journey label against the published journey.
