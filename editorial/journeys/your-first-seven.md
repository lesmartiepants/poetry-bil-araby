# Your First Seven · سبع سماوات

## The professor’s perspective

I’d begin by reading the first two words aloud. Imruʾ al-Qays addresses two people at once and asks them to stop with him. The first thing a reader learns about the Arabic ode is that it makes room for company. From there, I’d keep asking who is being addressed and what the poet needs that person to hear. A brother needs to be remembered, a patron needs to hear a grievance, and someone far away needs to answer a lover.

The journey gives a newcomer seven experiences worth remembering in their own right. It also gives them a reason to return to the first poem. When Ibn ʿAbd Rabbih corrects himself halfway through a line about exile, the opening campsite comes back to mind. A place can be empty while the person who remembers it is still divided between there and here.

**Journey intro**

> Seven poems begin with a plea to stop and weep and end with a poet caught between two places. Along the way, hear what happens when a bird, a cup of coffee, or the sound of rain carries more than a person can say outright. Start anywhere, or follow the seven in order.

## What to teach at each stop

### Heaven 1 · The abandoned camp

**Professor’s note.** Imruʾ al-Qays gives us an entry into the _qaṣīda_, the long classical ode, through a surprisingly small piece of evidence. Droppings that look like peppercorns sit where people used to live. Let the reader see that landscape before introducing the convention of the abandoned camp. The request to stop and weep prepares us for Al-Khansa addressing her own eye. [Ithra edition](https://www.ithra.com/files/6516/0984/6883/Al_Muallaqat.pdf) · [Al-Tibrizi recension](https://www.mouallakat.com/poems/imru-al-qays/)

**Reader cue.** The speaker asks two companions to stop with him. What can he still see at the camp after everyone else has gone?

### Heaven 2 · Grief

**Professor’s note.** Al-Khansa is a defining voice in _rithāʾ_, the Arabic elegy. A ring-necked bird brings Sakhr back to her, then the poem becomes a portrait of the brother she wants others to remember. The turn from private pain to the way someone is seen in public prepares the reader for the very different room in Al-Mutanabbi. [Arabic edition](https://uqu.edu.sa/App/Poetry/Poems/104058) · [Bilingual anthology](https://penguinrandomhousesecondaryeducation.com/book/?isbn=9780375712432)

**Reader cue.** A bird calls and Sakhr comes back to mind. Where does her grief become a portrait of him?

### Heaven 3 · Pride

**Professor’s note.** The excerpt starts with Al-Mutanabbi addressing a patron who is both his opponent and his judge. That predicament changes the sound of the famous boast. The list that brings horses, night, weapons, paper, and pen together is also a claim made in front of an audience that can withhold recognition. With Wallada, we’ll hear another speaker make a difficult request in her own voice. [Arabic text](https://www.aldiwan.net/poem20938.html)

**Reader cue.** Hold the opening reproach in mind when the horses, sword, paper, and pen arrive. What does he need the room to believe about him?

### Heaven 4 · Desire

**Professor’s note.** Wallada’s Córdoba lyric makes remembered winter visits feel hot with longing. I’d pause at the closing wish that rain fall on the beloved’s new home. The blessing lets concern survive separation and gives the reader a first meaning for rain before Al-Sayyab asks it to carry much more. Set this beside Ibn Zaydun’s _Poem in N_ (`41998`) for two voices addressing separation in the Andalusi lyric. [Bilingual anthology](https://penguinrandomhousesecondaryeducation.com/book/?isbn=9780375712432)

**Reader cue.** She remembers a winter that felt like burning coals and ends by wishing rain on another home. Where does the temperature of the poem change?

### Heaven 5 · Rain

**Professor’s note.** Al-Sayyab offers a way into modern Arabic free verse through something readers can hear at once. The excerpt brings hunger and harvest together, then repeats _maṭar_ until rain can carry tears and renewal as well. Compare that repeated beat with the opening ode’s couplets. The field also leaves us thinking about who gets to eat, a question Darwish brings indoors with bread. [SOAS study and translation](https://eprints.soas.ac.uk/33610/1/11010371.pdf) · [Hindawi edition](https://downloads.hindawi.org/books/79619585.pdf)

**Reader cue.** Rain follows the image of a harvest that fails to feed the people around it. How does the refrain feel by the time Iraq is imagined green?

### Heaven 6 · Home

**Professor’s note.** Darwish gives longing for Palestine the intimacy of an ordinary morning. Bread, coffee, and touch make it possible to feel an absence before knowing its history. The poem imagines returning in forms as humble as fuel for an oven or a line for laundry. It prepares the final poem’s question about which part of a person can go home. [Study of the poem](https://journals.ekb.eg/article_286997_b5763fcdb3e39ae8915913c2bb1d2b.pdf)

**Reader cue.** The mother’s home is made of small things a child would miss. As the poem goes on, imagine what it would mean to come back as one of them.

### Heaven 7 · Home, again

**Professor’s note.** Ibn ʿAbd Rabbih gives this journey an Andalusi miniature after the longer modern poems. In the second reading line, _bal_ turns a lament about the soul’s loneliness into a correction about the body’s exile. Two bayts leave room for a second reading on the spot. Put that act of revision beside Imruʾ al-Qays’s decision to stop at a place someone else might pass by. [Arabic text](https://ar.wikisource.org/wiki/الجسم_في_بلد_والروح_في_بلد)

**Reader cue.** The speaker laments the soul’s loneliness and changes his mind before the line is over. Which part of him seems furthest from home?
