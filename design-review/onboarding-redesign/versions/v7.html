Ten Forks and a Profile

Ten either/ors, one per screen, auto-advancing the moment you tap. Both sides are always genuinely appealing, neither names a category, and none of them needs you to have read a line of Arabic poetry. Answer ten or answer two — "That's enough" is live from the first screen, and an unanswered fork simply contributes nothing.

What the current flow gets wrong

Having tapped through it: not the craft — the hex comb, the bilingual weighting, the honest two doors are all better than most apps ship. The problem is upstream of the pixels. The flow spends its entire attention budget on self-report.

Six screens, no poem
The product's whole proposition is a line, set well — and the flow withholds it until after the sixth tap. Calm, Duolingo and Spotify all deliver a taste of the core emotion inside the first screen; this delivers a taxonomy quiz.
Declared ≠ revealed
"Which images stay with you?" asks a reader to already know their own taxonomy. People answer aspirationally. Meanwhile preferenceWeighting.js only needs labels — and a poem someone actually kept carries them more honestly than a checkbox.
Step 5 is a status test
"Easy reading, or a challenge?" makes a reader rank their own Arabic in public. It is the highest-abandonment shape a question can take, and the answer is inferable for free from reading posture plus whether they open a translation.
Locked doors
Both CTAs sit dimmed and inert until posture is picked, with no copy explaining the lock — friction placed at the exact moment the cost of leaving is lowest.
No payoff
The flow ends on navigate('/'). Six answers, and the reward is the identical screen a skipper gets. Nothing is shown to have changed, so nothing was worth answering.
Write-once, invisible
Answers never resurface. No "you're seeing this because you said grief", no one-tap correction — so a wrong answer is a permanent, unattributable tax on the feed.

The eight rules the new questions follow

Direction B's first pass kept the old questions and only re-laid them out, which fixed nothing. These are the rules the rewrite is actually built on — they apply to any question this app ever adds.

Ask about the reader, not the schema
"Where in time?" is a database column wearing a question mark. Nobody has an opinion about the 11th century. They have an opinion about how they want the next ten minutes to go.
Never require expertise the product is the only source of
You cannot prefer Abbasid over Andalusi before reading either. Asking anyway produces noise — and teaches the reader, on screen one, that they are unqualified to be here.
Never make the reader rank themselves
"Easy reading, or a challenge?" is a status test — it asks the reader to place themselves on a ladder. The same three buckets, asked as appetite, are not: "Keep it plain" / "A bit of work is fine" / "Give me the deep end". Nothing is phrased as a deficiency and nothing flatters the hard option, so every rung is a comfortable thing to pick.
Vary the shape
Four identical chip fields read as one long question and get answered with one long shrug. Cards, then a side-by-side comparison, then two quick chip rows — different cognitive modes, so the sheet has a rhythm instead of a length.
Constrain the answer
An unlimited multi-select carries almost no information — a reader who taps eight of eight moods has told you nothing. Single-select is what makes an answer informative.
Every question pays rent
If you can't name the column it moves, cut it. Occasion moves mood and intensity; the each fork moves named mood, motif or family keys; the ease question moves accessibility_score; the world moves poems.century. The table below names the column for every single one. Nothing is asked that the draw cannot use.
Teach by the answer, not the question
"From which age? — 9th–10th, Abbasid" is unanswerable if you don't already know what Abbasid means, which is most people. So the option you tap is a scene you can picture (Baghdad after dark — wine, wit, and the caliph's court) and the proper name sits under it, as the answer to a question you just asked yourself. You choose on feeling and leave knowing a word you arrived without — then meet it a second time on the reveal. Never make a term the price of admission.
"Surprise me" is an answer, not a skip
"Read me something I'd never find" sits in the same list as the rest and does real work — it raises the draw temperature. A reader who wants no profile gets to say so inside the flow rather than by leaving it.

How an answer becomes a query

The single most important constraint: an answer is a weight, not a filter. ANDing five predicates against 4,767 servable poems collapses the funnel to about fourteen results — the reader is punished for answering honestly. So the flow runs in two layers, and only a very small part of any answer becomes real SQL.

Layer 1

The candidate query

candidateQueries(prefs) issues two requests to /api/poems/by-category, always: one anchored page carrying at most a couple of predicates, and one open page carrying none. Merging them is what guarantees the first of the engine's two promises — nothing in the corpus is ever unreachable, however much you answered.

Layer 2

The score

Every other answer is a term in scorePoem, which grades each candidate out of 5.0 on how much of the profile it satisfies. sampleByScore then draws by softmax, so a 4-of-5 poem is genuinely likelier than a 1-of-5 one and neither is impossible. Temperature decays over poems seen: seed, then broaden.

The tally, exactly

Each fork carries the real taxonomy keys both sides imply. A tap scores +1.0 on every key the chosen side carries and −0.4 on the other side's — asymmetric on purpose, because "I'd rather read A" is not "I dislike B", it is "B lost one comparison". An unanswered fork contributes exactly zero, which is what makes "No preference" and "That's enough" honest rather than decorative. Keys still positive after ten forks are kept, strongest first, capped at 4 moods / 3 motifs / 1 family so a reader who answers everything doesn't hand the scorer a profile that weights everything and therefore nothing.

The output is the ordinary {family, moods, motifs, era, difficulty} payload that preferenceWeighting.js already consumes. The carousel is an input method, not a new engine — scorePoem, candidateQueries and the draw temperature are untouched. The reader never sees a taxonomy key; the engine never sees a would-you-rather.
