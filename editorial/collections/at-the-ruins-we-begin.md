# At the Ruins, We Begin · عند الأطلال نبدأ

## The professor’s perspective

Imruʾ al-Qays begins by asking two companions to stop and weep with him. Antarah begins by asking whether earlier poets have left anything for him to say. I’d put those openings side by side. One gives a reader company at an abandoned campsite. The other lets a reader hear a poet wondering what he can make of an inheritance that already feels crowded.

**What do we hear differently when we put a famous line back inside its poem?** Al-Mutanabbi’s horses and desert become witnesses in a quarrel with his patron. Al-Rundi’s maxim about decline becomes a lament for cities he names aloud. Al-Busiri brings the language of remembered love into an ode that people still recite together.

## What to teach in each poem

### 1 · Imruʾ al-Qays · Stop, Let Us Weep

**Professor’s note.** The very first verb addresses two people. Before the poet tells us what he has lost, he asks for company. At the campsite he notices antelope droppings small as peppercorns. The detail gives the companions something they can still see and the reader a way to stand beside them. [Arabic text and commentary](https://mouallakat.com/poems/imru-al-qays/)

**Reader cue.** The speaker asks two people to stop with him. What is still visible at the campsite?

### 2 · Antarah · The Muʿallaqa

**Professor’s note.** Antarah asks whether earlier poets have left a place in need of mending, then wonders whether he recognizes the beloved’s dwelling. That double hesitation makes the confidence of the later battle scenes worth hearing. One of the poem’s surprises is how easily the thought of Abla finds its way into an account of courage. [The ten odes with al-Tibrizi’s commentary](https://books.google.com/books/about/A_commentary_on_ten_ancient_Arabic_poems.html?id=8bcoAAAAYAAJ)

**Reader cue.** Mark the first time he addresses Abla or her home. When does she come back to mind?

### 3 · Al-Khansa · Is There Grit in Your Eye?

**Professor’s note.** Al-Khansa begins as if a weeping eye might have grit in it. The questions turn to a house emptied of its people, and her tears run as though the eye had become a stream. When she remembers how Sakhr gave freely when others held back, the loss becomes something his neighbors can feel too. [Arabic text, opening sixteen bayts](https://www.qawafi.org/poems/qadhan-bi-aynik)

**Reader cue.** Read the first question once as if someone had asked it in a room with you. What changes when the poem answers?

### 4 · Zuhayr · Echoes of a Lost Home

**Professor’s note.** The weary line comes well into an ode that opens at the traces of Umm Awfa’s dwelling and spends time praising those who make peace. When the speaker says eighty years will wear anyone out, the advice that follows has a body and a history behind it. [The ten odes with al-Tibrizi’s commentary](https://books.google.com/books/about/A_commentary_on_ten_ancient_Arabic_poems.html?id=8bcoAAAAYAAJ)

**Reader cue.** Read the admission of weariness beside one of the judgments that follow it. What does age seem to have sharpened for him?

### 5 · Tarafa · The Muʿallaqa

**Professor’s note.** Khawla’s traces look like a tattoo showing through the skin of a hand. Starting with that tiny mark makes the great length of the camel description feel deliberate. A young speaker who knows that time can take everything still has time to notice just how his animal moves. [The ten odes with al-Tibrizi’s commentary](https://books.google.com/books/about/A_commentary_on_ten_ancient_Arabic_poems.html?id=8bcoAAAAYAAJ)

**Reader cue.** Keep the tattoo in mind when the poem starts attending to the camel. Which details would you want to keep if you expected to lose them?

### 6 · Attributed to al-Shanfara · The L-Poem of the Arabs

**Professor’s note.** The speaker tells his kin to turn their mounts because he is leaving. Then he names wild animals as another family. That act of choosing kin is what I’d linger over. His independence also has rules about whom he trusts and how he lives with hunger. [Arabic text](https://islamarchive.cc/poems_show/8986)

**Reader cue.** Pay attention to what he admires in his new companions. What does he seem to have wanted from his old ones?

### 7 · Al-Mutanabbi · Wā Ḥarra Qalbāh

**Professor’s note.** Horses, night, and the desert know who he is. So do the paper and pen he names in the same breath. In the full poem he makes that boast while accusing Sayf al-Dawla of being both opponent and judge. I’d ask what it costs the speaker to need recognition from someone he is also accusing.

**Reader cue.** Read the famous line after the address to the judge. Who is being asked to bear witness now?

### 8 · Al-Mutanabbi · The Resolve of Men and the Heights of Virtue

**Professor’s note.** The first couplet measures resolve by the people who exercise it. The next gives small and great people different measures for the same deeds. In an ode praising Sayf al-Dawla, that play with scale tells the audience whose judgment the poet thinks is worth having.

**Reader cue.** How does the second couplet change the way you hear the first?

### 9 · Al-Mutanabbi · A World Without Anchor

**Professor’s note.** The speaker counts up what he lacks. Family and homeland come first, then a drinking companion, a cup, and a place to stay. Beside the preceding odes, the list gives us another side of the poet who could ask the whole desert to recognize him.

**Reader cue.** Where does the list become intimate for you?

### 10 · Abu Tammam · The Sword Speaks Truer Than Books

**Professor’s note.** The books in the opening belong to a contest over who gets to say what will happen. The poem celebrates the capture of Amorium and makes the victory an answer to people who tried to read the outcome in the stars. The poet makes an event do the work of an argument. [Arabic text](https://poetry.dct.gov.ae/poems/1118-السيف-أصدق-أنباء-من-الكتب)

**Reader cue.** Look for the places where the poem makes you see something happening and the places where someone claims to know what will happen.

### 11 · Abu Nuwas · The Wine of Solace

**Professor’s note.** The speaker tells his critic that blame makes the temptation stronger, then asks to be cured with the very thing that made him sick. The golden wine that follows would cheer up a stone. I’d let readers hear the joke before asking how he turns someone else’s moral advice into material for his own pleasure.

**Reader cue.** Read the first couplet as a reply to someone you can almost hear speaking. What tone do you give the reply?

### 12 · Al-Maʿarri · Of No Avail, in My Belief

**Professor’s note.** The elegy asks whether a dove is mourning or singing. A sound the poet hears without being able to explain gives way to an image of the earth crowded with the dead. The poem lets doubt change how a person listens and how lightly they put a foot down. [Study of the elegy](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004499287/BP000005.xml?language=en)

**Reader cue.** Try to decide what the dove is doing. What in the poem makes you reconsider?

### 13 · Abu Firas · I See You Holding Back Your Tears

**Professor’s note.** The opening speaks to someone whose habit is to endure. The reply admits to longing and says someone like him keeps his secrets. Then night arrives, and he describes tears in the language of a struggle for command. I’d bring Al-Khansa back here to hear another poet ask what an eye can give away. [Arabic text](https://diwan.sy/poem/arak-sy-aldam)

**Reader cue.** Where does his account of what he feels begin to sound different from the picture in the first line?

### 14 · Ibn al-Rumi · The Heart of the Necklace

**Professor’s note.** The father tells his eyes that crying can soothe him even when it can do nothing for his son. He then imagines the child as the central bead of a necklace, the one death picked out. Beside Al-Khansa’s elegy, this poem gives readers a second way to notice how grief changes the meaning of a familiar object.

**Reader cue.** What does the small bead let the father say about where his son belonged?

### 15 · Ibn Zaydun · Poem in N

**Professor’s note.** The opening replaces nearness with distance, and the same Arabic ending keeps bringing back a first-person plural. The couple can still be a “we” in the sound of a sentence about being apart. This is one reason to hear the Arabic alongside the English.

**Reader cue.** Notice how often the rhyme carries the sound of “us” or “our.” Where does that sound matter most?

### 16 · Al-Rundi · The Perfection of Imperfection

**Professor’s note.** Al-Rundi begins with a maxim that could apply to anyone. Once he asks where the cities of al-Andalus have gone, the question has places and people inside it. Read the opener again after the cities have been named and see what the word “decline” has acquired. [Arabic text](https://adabedia.com/poem/لكل-شيء-إذا-ما-تم-نقصان)

**Reader cue.** Find the first question that names a city. Does the opening maxim sound as certain after it?

### 17 · Al-Busiri · The Mantle

**Professor’s note.** The ode begins by asking whether remembered neighbors have brought tears to the speaker’s eyes. The opening gives us a gesture we already know from the campsite and Al-Khansa’s elegy. In Al-Busiri’s praise of the Prophet Muhammad, that language of longing becomes part of a devotional poem people still recite together. [British Library manuscript catalogue](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/032-003475282) · [Asil Ensemble performance](https://www.mustafasaid.co/burda)

**Reader cue.** Keep the opening question in mind when the poem turns to praise. What happens to the speaker’s longing?
