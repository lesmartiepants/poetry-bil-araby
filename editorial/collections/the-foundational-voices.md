# The Foundational Voices · أصوات التأسيس

## The professor’s perspective

I’d start with two moments from Imruʾ al-Qays. In two couplets, he brings together wine, rain, lavender and honey before telling us what they remind him of. In his great ode, he asks two companions to stop with him where someone he loved once lived. One poem lets desire fill the senses. The other asks what desire can still find when the place is empty.

**What can a poem hold when a person, a place, or a way of life is slipping away?** Al-Khansa hears her brother return in a dove’s call. Tarafa treats his days as something borrowed, while Labid imagines what those years do to the body. Antarah wants to be seen by the woman he loves and by the people who change what they call him when they need him. That question of who gets to claim whom carries us from Zuhayr’s offer of peace and Amr’s challenge to a king to Al-Shanfara’s refusal of burial.

The Muʿallaqāt (المعلّقات), the pre-Islamic odes traditionally taught in groups of seven or ten, give the collection its longest reading. Open Imruʾ al-Qays at the campsite, then see how much one poem can hold as it travels through remembered desire, a long night, a galloping horse and a storm. [Al-Tibrizi’s commentary on the ten odes, edited by Charles James Lyall](https://books.google.com/books/about/A_commentary_on_ten_ancient_Arabic_poems.html?id=8bcoAAAAYAAJ)

## What to teach in each poem

### 1 · Imruʾ al-Qays · Nectar's Wine, Honey's Melt

**Professor’s note.** The Tonight pick gives readers Imruʾ al-Qays’s sensory precision in two couplets. The poem names wine, rain, lavender and honey before it names the coolness of the beloved’s teeth. That delay lets a whole landscape collect around one intimate detail. [Arabic text](https://www.aldiwan.net/poem97597.html)

**Reader cue.** Read the opening list before looking at the next line. How does the shift from rain and lavender to the coolness of her teeth change what you pictured?

### 2 · Imruʾ al-Qays · The Mu’allaqa of Imru’ al-Qays

**Professor’s note.** The opening verb, قفا, asks two people to stop. I’d start there because the speaker wants company at the place where the beloved once lived. Then I’d follow the poem’s sense of motion. A horse hurtles like a boulder brought down by a torrent, and water eventually returns in the storm itself. The complete ode lets readers feel the distance between the opening halt and that rush. [Arabic text and commentary](https://mouallakat.com/)

**Reader cue.** At the campsite, even the antelope droppings come into focus. When you reach the horse and the storm, see how quickly the poem makes your eye move.

### 3 · Al-Khansa · Lament for a Brother

**Professor’s note.** Al-Khansa asks her own eye to weep for Sakhr, then hears a dove and finds the memory of him has arrived on its own. When she turns to address him directly, she remembers his generosity down to the animals he gave away. That particularity is one of the powers of her elegy. The person she misses becomes someone a stranger can begin to picture. [Thaʿlab’s edition of Al-Khansa’s diwan](https://uqu.edu.sa/en/App/Poetry/Poems/104058)

**Reader cue.** Follow the poem from the dove in the branches to the moment she speaks to Sakhr himself. What can she say once he becomes “you”?

### 4 · Tarafa ibn al-Abd · Death's Indifference, Time a Loan

**Professor’s note.** I’d introduce Tarafa through the impatience of a young person who wants to know what to do with the time he has. The poem begins with death leading someone by a halter. Then it calls our days a loan and tells us to take provisions from them. The two images give urgency a physical form. [Arabic text](https://poetsgate.com/poem.php?pm=37503)

**Reader cue.** Pause on the idea that your days are borrowed. What does the poem want you to carry away from them?

### 5 · Labid ibn Rabiʿa · The Weary Burden of Years

**Professor’s note.** Labid, whose life spanned the emergence of Islam, gives long life two textures. First comes a tender branch that still bends in the wind. Then there is an arrow shaft stripped of feathers, beyond repair. Placed after Tarafa, the poem lets us hear what limited time sounds like from the far end of a long life. [Arabic text](https://www.aldiwan.net/poem21275.html)

**Reader cue.** Follow the wood from the branch to the worn arrow. What has happened to its ability to move?

### 6 · Antarah ibn Shaddad · Chiding Time, Kin's Deceit

**Professor’s note.** I’d introduce the warrior-poet through his longing for Abla. He wishes her image would come at night and see him cry. Earlier, his own people call him the son of Zabiba in peace and the son of noble men when the horses clash. The change of name shows the terms on which they grant him recognition. His wish that Abla could see him makes room for a different kind of recognition. [Arabic diwan text](https://uqu.edu.sa/App/Poetry/Poems/1905)

**Reader cue.** Notice the two names his people give him. When he imagines Abla seeing him at night, what does he want her to witness?

### 7 · Zuhayr ibn Abi Sulma · Tribal Pride and War

**Professor’s note.** Zuhayr puts a bargain for peace beside the image of war biting down. He invokes ties of kinship and reminds the other side of what his people can do in battle. I’d read the offer aloud twice to hear how a poet known for weighing the costs of war makes room for a settlement while both parties still have their pride. [Arabic text](https://www.aldiwan.net/poem25052.html)

**Reader cue.** Find the line that offers peace. How do the lines around it make the offer sound to the people receiving it?

### 8 · Amr ibn Kulthum · Shadow of Injustice, Broken Trust

**Professor’s note.** Amr starts by asking someone to carry his words to the king, then addresses the king directly. Once the speaker begins saying “we,” the grievance becomes a claim about what an entire people is owed for standing beside its ruler. That is where I’d make room to hear the collective pride for which Amr’s verse is known. [Corpus text](https://poetry-bil-araby-2mb0.onrender.com/api/poems/62296)

**Reader cue.** Where does the messenger disappear from the poem? Read that turn again and hear how close the king suddenly seems.

### 9 · Al-Nabigha al-Dhubyani · The Prince's Fate

**Professor’s note.** Al-Nabigha starts with a question to a man who can tell him what is happening inside. As he imagines the ruler dead, the loss becomes the loss of spring and of the sacred month. I’d put this beside Amr to ask what someone standing close to power can imagine surviving its disappearance. [Corpus text](https://poetry-bil-araby-2mb0.onrender.com/api/poems/76804)

**Reader cue.** The speaker asks for news at a gate. Follow his imagination past the gate and see how far the possible loss spreads.

### 10 · Al-Shanfara · A Strange Testament

**Professor’s note.** Al-Shanfara gives the collection’s outsider the first word over his own burial. The command arrives before we know what has happened to him. Coming after poems about who will honor or mourn the dead, it makes us ask what it would mean to refuse the care a community normally gives. [Arabic text in Abu al-Faraj al-Isfahani’s _Kitab al-Aghani_](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/1701/120?lang=ar)

**Reader cue.** Read the opening aloud as an order. Who is still being addressed, even in a poem that begins by refusing their help?
