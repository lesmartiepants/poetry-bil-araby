# Women Who Wrote the Canon · نساء كتبن الديوان

## The professor’s perspective

I’d open by putting Al-Khansa and Fadwa Tuqan beside each other. Al-Khansa asks her eye to weep for Sakhr. At the gates of Jaffa, Tuqan asks both eyes to stop and weep at ruined houses, echoing the opening of Imruʾ al-Qays’s ode. Once a reader hears those old gestures in a new city, the canon starts to feel like something poets work with. [Close reading of Tuqan and the classical tradition](https://knowledge.uchicago.edu/record/13676/files/Palestine-Futurity-and-the-Rith%C4%81%CA%BE.pdf)

The collection asks **what have women poets made possible in Arabic verse?** Let readers encounter the poems in different registers. Layla al-Akhyaliyya makes grief a public act of remembering. ʿUlayya can make longing move up and down in two couplets, then build an entire remembered landscape out of sound. Wallada makes room for swagger and vulnerability. Nazik al-Malaika makes a public calamity audible, and Iman Mersal makes an ordinary conversation about a house into a poem. Each encounter gives a beginner a way to notice a choice the poet made.

**Collection intro**

> A bird calls a brother back to mind. A poet in Córdoba claims the right to give her own kiss. A poet in Baghdad makes the sound of an epidemic travel through the night. Read these twelve poems for the ways women have made grief, desire, faith, and public memory speak in Arabic.

## What to teach in each poem

### 1 · Al-Khansa — Lament for a Brother

**Professor’s note.** Al-Khansa gives readers an early model of the elegy through something wonderfully involuntary. A bird calls and the memory of a brother arrives before she can summon it. She goes on to decide what about Sakhr deserves to become public memory. [Arabic edition](https://uqu.edu.sa/App/Poetry/Poems/104058)

**Reader cue.** Notice what the bird brings back, then what the poet chooses to tell us about her brother.

### 2 · Layla al-Akhyaliyya — An Elegy for Tawba

**Professor’s note.** The Umayyad poet begins with an oath and asks what death can do to a person’s honor. The argument is part of the grief. She is deciding how Tawba is to be judged once he can no longer speak for himself. [Arabic edition and variants](https://uqu.edu.sa/en/App/Poetry/Poems/100923)

**Reader cue.** The poem opens with a vow. Follow it into the speaker’s argument about what makes a life honorable.

### 3 · Layla al-Akhyaliyya — Weep for Tawba

**Professor’s note.** Reading two elegies for Tawba teaches us to hear two different acts of mourning by the same poet. This one asks the women of Khafaja to weep and remembers the people Tawba answered when they called for help. The invitation grows wider as the portrait becomes more particular. [Classical anthology](https://adab.wiki/wiki/منتهى_الطلب_من_أشعار_العرب/ليلى_الأخيلية)

**Reader cue.** The first tears are hers. See how many other people the poem gives a reason to mourn.

### 4 · Attributed to Rabiʿa al-Adawiyya — Two Loves

**Professor’s note.** The verses traditionally associated with the early Sufi figure Rabiʿa let readers hear the intimate address of a love poem as devotion. The speaker describes one love through the work of remembering and the other through what the beloved allows her to see. I’d let that difference emerge before introducing the word _Sufi_. [Study and Arabic text](https://air.unimi.it/retrieve/dfa8b997-eb18-748b-e053-3a05fe0a3a96/8354-24857-1-PB.pdf)

**Reader cue.** The same person is loved in two ways. Watch who does the work in each half of the poem.

### 5 · ʿUlayya bint al-Mahdi — Longing, Tears, and Hope’s Ascent

**Professor’s note.** ʿUlayya was a poet and musician of the Abbasid court. In this short lyric, tears flow and dry, desire rises, and despair pulls the speaker back to a seat. The changing direction gives us a way to feel the lyric’s movement in four reading lines. [Study of ʿUlayya’s poetry and music](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/pub/sirnakifd/article/1234234)

**Reader cue.** Hope lifts the speaker and despair pulls her down. Consider how much happens to the body in four lines.

### 6 · ʿUlayya bint al-Mahdi — Farewell to the Caravan

**Professor’s note.** The second ʿUlayya selection gives a reader time to settle into a scene. Drowsy travelers and the clamor of camel drivers lead into the remembered clicking and birdsong of a greener place. The two poems together make a case for her range even within the experience of longing. [Study of ʿUlayya’s poetry and music](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/pub/sirnakifd/article/1234234)

**Reader cue.** The sounds of the road give way to the sounds of home. Notice which place she can make you hear most clearly.

### 7 · Wallada bint al-Mustakfi — By God, I Was Made for High Things

**Professor’s note.** Wallada gives the collection a sudden change of posture. She announces what she was made for, then shows us how she walks and whom she chooses to kiss. In four lines, the Córdoba poet makes self-possession feel like a physical action. [Bilingual anthology](https://penguinrandomhousesecondaryeducation.com/book/?isbn=9780375712432)

**Reader cue.** Begin with her walk. How much of the poem’s confidence is already there before she offers a kiss?

### 8 · Wallada bint al-Mustakfi — Must Separation Mean We Have No Way to Meet?

**Professor’s note.** The second Wallada makes the first richer. The poet who can give a kiss now has to imagine a meeting. Winter still burns in her memory, and the blessing of rain for the beloved’s home leaves an affection more complicated than a complaint. [Bilingual anthology](https://penguinrandomhousesecondaryeducation.com/book/?isbn=9780375712432)

**Reader cue.** The poem begins by asking for a meeting and ends by wishing rain on another home. What can a blessing say from that distance?

### 9 · Nazik al-Malaika — Cholera

**Professor’s note.** Start with the stillness. Then there are groans, footsteps, and a returning word for death that makes the page feel crowded with sound. The Iraqi poet and critic offers a way to experience modern Arabic free verse through what its changing rhythm can make a reader hear. [Arabic teaching edition](https://elearning.univ-mila.dz/a2026/pluginfile.php/77525/mod_resource/content/0/نموذج%20تطبيقي%20حول%20قصيدة%20التّفعيلة.pdf) · [Author and publication background](https://museum.uobaghdad.edu.iq/?page_id=15098)

**Reader cue.** The night is quiet at first. Notice how repetition makes the sounds of mourning harder to keep at a distance.

### 10 · Fadwa Tuqan — I Will Not Cry

**Professor’s note.** Tuqan begins at ruined houses in Jaffa by echoing the opening of the classical ode and turning its appeal toward her own eyes. The title makes the tears she asks for at the start a question the rest of the poem has to answer. Bring Al-Khansa back here so the reader can hear two poets asking their eyes to do something with loss. [Original _al-Adab_ issue](https://libraries.aub.edu.lb/blacklight/catalog/b17782314) · [Close reading](https://knowledge.uchicago.edu/record/13676/files/Palestine-Futurity-and-the-Rith%C4%81%CA%BE.pdf)

**Reader cue.** Set the title beside the first plea to weep. What changes between the moment she stands at the ruins and the way she speaks at the end?

### 11 · Fadwa Tuqan — Life’s Wandering in the Wilderness

**Professor’s note.** Give the reader a second Tuqan with a different scale of attention. In the beginning, a lone walker can hear only her own steps. A meeting changes what the road means, and by the closing lines the sound can travel as song. The repeated walking gives the change something to push against.

**Reader cue.** At first the only footsteps are her own. Notice when the road begins to sound different.

### 12 · Iman Mersal — The Idea of Home

**Professor’s note.** Mersal gives the collection a living Egyptian voice and changes the shape of the page with five prose blocks. A lost key, a broker’s view of a house, and the stairs someone knows by heart suggest three kinds of belonging. The last image gives readers a wall worth rereading beside the ruined dwellings in Tuqan. [Arabic text and author’s reading](https://www.lyrikline.org/ar/9833/10372) · [The Threshold](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9780374607852/thethreshold/)

**Reader cue.** The key keeps disappearing while three people imagine what a house can do for them. Which of their ideas can you still believe by the last image?
